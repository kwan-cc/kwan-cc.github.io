Cross-Sector Disruption: How Smartphone Giants are Reshaping Humanoid Robotics

May 05, 2026

Recapping the Core Event: Honor D1's Half-Marathon Victory

In April 2026, a surprising phenomenon occurred at the Beijing E-Town Robot Half Marathon: Honor's humanoid robots, D1 and Lightning, not only defeated professional robotics companies like Unitree but also completed the race in 50 minutes and 26 seconds, beating the human world record by a full 7 minutes [1]. This victory was not just a technological showcase; it symbolized a powerful cross-sector invasion of the robotics field by the smartphone industry.

Honor engineer Yao Bin revealed after the race that the key to the robots maintaining high-intensity running for extended periods was the application of smartphone cooling technology. In traditional robots, motor overheating often limits performance under high loads. Smartphone manufacturers, with their experience in managing high-heat chips in confined spaces, provided a novel solution for robotics [2].

Industry Background: Smartphone Market Saturation vs. Robotics Growth

The collective shift of the smartphone supply chain towards humanoid robots is primarily driven by drastic changes in the market environment. According to IDC, global smartphone shipments are projected to face a historic decline of 12.9% in 2026 [3]. As the smartphone market enters an era of highly saturated "stock competition," seeking a second growth curve has become a survival consensus for supply chain enterprises.

Concurrently, the humanoid robot market is on the cusp of a boom. Counterpoint Research predicts that by 2027, the global humanoid robot market will exceed 100,000 units, a six-fold increase from just 16,000 units in 2025 [4]. This potential for growth, from "tens of millions" to "hundreds of millions," has attracted numerous precision manufacturers who previously served Apple, Huawei, and Xiaomi.

Technological Overlap: The "Dimensionality Reduction Attack" Advantage

Smartphone manufacturers' entry into the robotics field is not a blind cross-over but is based on deep technological overlap and capacity reuse.

Technology Transfer Roadmap

The following diagram illustrates how core smartphone technologies are being adapted and scaled for humanoid robot applications:

Core Component Smartphone Technology Foundation Humanoid Robot Application Scenarios
Sensing Systems Multi-camera modules, LiDAR, MEMS sensors Environmental perception, navigation and obstacle avoidance, spatial modeling
Power & Energy High-energy-density batteries, precise power management Joint actuation, long-endurance power systems
Interaction & Feedback Haptic motors, voice AI processing Bionic hand haptic feedback, human-robot voice interaction
Precision Structures Ultra-thin body assembly, precision hinges (foldable phones) Robot joints, skeletal structural components
Thermal Management VC vapor chambers, graphene cooling, active cooling Motor and core computing unit heat dissipation

This high degree of technological commonality allows smartphone suppliers such as Lingyi iTech, Lens Technology, and AAC Technologies to rapidly adjust their production capacity [5]. For instance, Lingyi iTech has established a joint venture with AgiBot and built a super factory in Beijing, aiming for an annual production capacity of 500,000 units by 2030 [6].

Giant's Layout: From Labs to Factory Floors

Currently, Chinese smartphone giants have adopted three distinct strategic paths in the robotics field:

  1. Industrial Deployment (Xiaomi): Xiaomi's CyberOne V2 is actively being deployed on its own car factory assembly lines, utilizing robots for precise part gripping and installation [7].

  2. Ecosystem Investment (Huawei): Huawei has deeply invested in underlying technologies by backing AgiBot, founded by former Huawei "genius youth" Zhihui Jun, and establishing a 3 billion RMB robotics subsidiary [8].

  3. R&D Empowerment (OPPO): OPPO focuses on applying robotics technology to internal automated testing and R&D processes to improve product iteration efficiency [9].

Challenges and Outlook: The Distance from "Running" to "Being Useful"

Despite the strong manufacturing advantages, the commercialization of humanoid robots still faces significant challenges. Firstly, the requirements for precision and reliability are far higher than for consumer electronics. Secondly, cost control is crucial; how to reduce the cost of robots to an affordable level for the general public will be key to future competition.

"This is not a simple transformation, but an extension of capacity and technology." Industry analysts point out that the entry of the smartphone supply chain will greatly accelerate the standardization and scaling of humanoid robots [4].
