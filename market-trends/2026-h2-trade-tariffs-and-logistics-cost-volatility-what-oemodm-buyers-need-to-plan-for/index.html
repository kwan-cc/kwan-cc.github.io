2026 H2 Trade Tariffs and Logistics Cost Volatility: What OEM/ODM Buyers Need to Plan For

Jul 16, 2026

For international buyers managing OEM ODM sourcing across Asia Pacific, the second half of 2026 brings a familiar but sharpening challenge. Trade tariffs 2026 policy shifts are stacking on top of ocean and air freight markets that swing hard in both directions within a single quarter. Procurement teams that priced risk once a year are finding that approach no longer holds. This article looks at where tariff policy stands heading into H2, what the latest freight data shows about logistics cost volatility, and how OEM/ODM buyers and their manufacturing partners are adjusting sourcing and contract strategy in response.

Trade Tariffs 2026: Where Policy Stands Heading Into H2

The tariff landscape going into the second half of 2026 is defined less by a single new rate and more by legal and procedural uncertainty. Earlier this year, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) does not authorize the president to impose tariffs on that legal basis, according to analysis from Elliott Davis. That ruling has pushed many importers into a dual track: pursuing refunds on duties already paid while simultaneously assessing exposure under whatever replacement tariff authority the administration turns to next. Elliott Davis notes that a temporary 10 percent import surcharge introduced in February 2026 is set to expire on July 24, 2026, making that date a real planning milestone rather than a footnote for buyers running landed-cost models this quarter.

Section 301 tariffs on Chinese-origin goods remain in force across a wide band, ranging from roughly 25 percent up to more than 100 percent on strategic categories such as electronics, textiles, and EV-related components, based on figures compiled by GrowthHQ. Enforcement is also tightening. Legal commentary flags 2026 as a "year of enforcement," with expanded government scrutiny of tariff evasion, transshipment, and misdeclared country-of-origin claims. For OEM/ODM buyers, this means the compliance bar for proving legitimate manufacturing origin, not just final assembly location, is rising at the same time tariff rates themselves stay unsettled.

The practical effect on sourcing decisions has been described by several industry advisors as a shift in the core question buyers ask their suppliers. Instead of "which option is cheaper," procurement teams are now asking "which option is more reliable and sustainable" over a multi-year horizon, a framing echoed across recent manufacturing tariff commentary. That shift matters because tariff policy in 2026 has proven it can move faster than most sourcing contracts are built to absorb.

Logistics Cost Volatility: The Data Behind the Swings

Tariff uncertainty would be easier to manage on its own. Layered onto genuinely volatile freight markets, it becomes a compounding risk. The ocean freight data from early 2026 tells a clear story of a market that has cooled from its 2024 peak but has not become predictable.

According to S&P Global Market Intelligence's 2026 ocean freight outlook, global container markets entered the year on very different footing than the prior two years, with spot rates down more than 70 percent from their 2024 peak amid vessel overcapacity and softening demand.

That is a meaningful drop from 2024 peak levels, but S&P Global is explicit that volatility "isn't going anywhere," pointing to port congestion, geopolitical routing risk, and carrier capacity discipline as ongoing pressure points.

One structural factor keeps distorting the picture on the Asia-Europe lane specifically: since late 2023, nearly all container traffic that once transited the Suez Canal has been rerouted around the Cape of Good Hope, adding roughly 3,500 nautical miles per voyage and absorbing an estimated 9 percent of global container fleet capacity, per S&P Global's analysis. At the same time, an order book of more than 800 new container ships, representing over 7 million TEUs, has been delivered into the market between 2024 and 2026, producing a level of structural overcapacity not seen since 2016. The combination of rerouted capacity absorption and new vessel supply is precisely why rates can swing sharply even without a new tariff headline.

Air freight tells a similarly choppy story. Data from Xeneta shows global air cargo spot rates rose 41 percent year on year by May 2026, reaching $3.40 per kilogram, after capacity reductions tied to Middle East disruptions and fuel costs that were up around 100 percent year on year earlier in the year. Contract behavior has shifted too: in the second quarter of 2026, 22 percent of new air freight contracts were signed for one month or less, more than double the 9 percent share seen in the same period of 2025, with more than half of forwarder-airline contracts now running under 30 days, levels not seen since the pandemic. The International Air Transport Association (IATA) still projects air cargo traffic growth of around 2.6 percent for 2026, meaning volume is stable even as pricing behavior has become far more short-term and reactive.

For OEM/ODM buyers, the practical takeaway from this data is that logistics cost volatility is now a persistent planning variable, not a temporary anomaly to wait out. Landed cost, not just quoted freight rate, has become the metric that matters, since insurance, longer routings, buffer inventory, and compliance overhead are all moving pieces feeding into the real cost of a shipment.

Supply Chain Strategy for OEM/ODM Buyers and Procurement Teams

Given a tariff environment still working through legal challenges and a freight market that reprices monthly rather than annually, procurement teams across Asia Pacific manufacturing networks are converging on a few concrete adjustments.

Build in dual or multi-sourcing by design, not as a contingency. Industry advisory sources describe a widely adopted "China+1" pattern, where companies diversify roughly 20 to 30 percent of production to an alternate site while retaining scale advantages at their primary manufacturing base. Vietnam and India are the two most cited beneficiaries of this shift, with Vietnam strengthening its position in electronics assembly and India drawing new investment on the back of government manufacturing incentives, according to sourcing analysis from GrowthHQ and IndustryIDX. Taiwan-based OEM/ODM manufacturers continue to play a distinct role in this picture, particularly where design ownership, component-level engineering, and higher-value subassembly work sit upstream of final assembly done elsewhere in the region.

Reassess nearshoring and friend-shoring on a category-by-category basis, not as a blanket policy. Not every product line benefits equally from moving assembly closer to end markets. Categories with high tariff exposure or complex compliance requirements are the better candidates for near-term diversification, while categories with thin margins and established supplier relationships may not justify the transition cost yet.

Rewrite contract terms to share tariff and freight risk explicitly. Fixed-price purchase orders that assume stable freight costs are increasingly out of step with a market where a third of contracts reprice monthly. Buyers are adding tariff pass-through clauses, indexed freight surcharges, and shorter contract terms that match the volatility they are actually seeing, rather than absorbing or externalizing all of the risk in one direction.

Treat inventory buffering as a cost-benefit decision, not a reflex. Holding extra safety stock protects against freight and customs disruption but ties up working capital and warehousing cost. The current environment favors selective buffering on high-tariff-exposure or long-lead-time categories rather than blanket inventory increases across the full product portfolio.

Track total landed cost and delivery reliability as the core procurement KPI, replacing a narrower focus on unit price or quoted freight rate alone. This aligns sourcing decisions with the real cost drivers reshaping Asia Pacific manufacturing today, from tariff classification risk to routing disruption to contract volatility.

FAQ

How much have ocean freight rates actually changed in 2026? Based on the Drewry World Container Index cited by S&P Global Market Intelligence, spot rates for a 40-foot container stood at $2,123 in mid-March 2026, down more than 70 percent from 2024 peak levels of $6,840 to $8,113 per FEU on major transpacific lanes. However, S&P Global cautions that the underlying market remains structurally volatile due to overcapacity, rerouted Asia-Europe traffic, and shifting trade policy, so current lower rates should not be read as a stable new baseline.

Should OEM/ODM buyers lock in long-term freight contracts during H2 2026? The data suggests caution around long-term commitments right now. Xeneta reports that 22 percent of new air freight contracts in Q2 2026 were signed for one month or less, more than double the share a year earlier, and more than half of forwarder-airline agreements now run under 30 days. This trend reflects a market where both carriers and shippers prefer flexibility over long-term rate certainty, which is a signal worth mirroring in procurement contract strategy for both ocean and air modes.

Which Asia Pacific sourcing locations are gaining ground because of tariff pressure? Vietnam and India are the most frequently cited beneficiaries of diversification away from single-country sourcing, according to industry sourcing analysis, with Vietnam expanding electronics assembly capacity and India drawing investment supported by government manufacturing incentives. Taiwan-based OEM/ODM manufacturers remain central to the region's higher-value design and component engineering work, often feeding into final assembly operations located elsewhere in the region.

Conclusion

Heading into the second half of 2026, OEM/ODM buyers are operating in a market where tariff policy and logistics cost volatility reinforce each other rather than moving independently. Legal uncertainty around U.S. tariff authority, an approaching surcharge expiration date, and tightening enforcement all sit on top of a freight market that reprices on a monthly rather than annual cycle. The organizations managing this well are not waiting for stability to return. They are building dual-sourcing capacity across Taiwan, Vietnam, and India, rewriting contracts to share risk explicitly, buffering inventory selectively rather than broadly, and shifting procurement KPIs toward total landed cost. None of this eliminates volatility, but it changes how exposed a buyer's supply chain strategy is when the next policy or rate shift arrives.
