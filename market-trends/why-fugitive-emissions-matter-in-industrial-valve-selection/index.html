Why Fugitive Emissions Matter in Industrial Valve Selection

Jun 26, 2026

Industrial valve selection has traditionally centered on pressure class, temperature range, materials, flow characteristics, and corrosion resistance. Those factors remain essential, but fugitive-emissions performance is moving much closer to the top of the specification sheet.

Fugitive emissions are unintended releases of gases or vapors from pressurized equipment. In valves, the main external leak paths are usually the stem or shaft seal and body joints. Leakage may be too small to see, yet across a plant containing hundreds or thousands of valves, many small releases can contribute to product loss, worker exposure, odor complaints, volatile organic compound emissions, or methane emissions.

A valve may therefore control flow successfully while still being unsuitable for a service where atmospheric leakage must be minimized.

Why the Issue Is Receiving More Attention

Regulation is one major driver. Leak detection and repair programs have existed for decades in refining, chemical processing, and oil and gas operations. More recent rules and reporting frameworks place greater emphasis on methane and other unintended releases. The European Union’s methane regulation, U.S. environmental requirements for oil and natural gas facilities, and UNEP’s Oil & Gas Methane Partnership 2.0 all point in a similar direction: emissions should be identified, measured more accurately, repaired, and documented.

Economics also matters. Escaping media may be saleable gas, process feedstock, refrigerant, steam, or a specialty chemical. The cumulative loss across a large asset can be significant, while detection, repeat maintenance, shutdowns, and replacement labor may exceed the initial price difference between valve options.

Fugitive emissions also connect several departments. A valve decision can affect emissions inventories, inspection workload, plant availability, safety management, and environmental targets. Selection is increasingly a cross-functional decision rather than a procurement choice based mainly on purchase price.

Where Valve Emissions Commonly Originate

For rising-stem valves such as many gate and globe valves, the packing system is a primary potential leak path. It must seal against pressure while allowing the stem to move. Repeated cycling, temperature changes, vibration, incorrect gland loading, stem damage, and material degradation can reduce performance.

Quarter-turn valves use different stem motion and sealing arrangements, but they are not automatically emission-free. Shaft seals and body connections still require suitable design, assembly, and maintenance.

External fugitive emissions should also be distinguished from internal seat leakage. Media passing across a closed seat remains inside the piping system, so it is usually an isolation problem rather than an atmospheric-emissions problem. It may, however, lead to emissions when the downstream side is an open vent, drain, or sampling line.

What Low-Emission Standards Tell Buyers

Recognized standards provide a more consistent basis for comparison. ISO 15848-1 covers type testing and classification of external leakage from valve stem or shaft seals and body joints. ISO 15848-2 addresses production acceptance testing when fugitive-emissions requirements are specified.

Within the API system, API 622 evaluates process-valve packing, API 624 applies to rising-stem valves equipped with graphite packing, and API 641 covers quarter-turn valves. These standards differ in scope, test conditions, mechanical cycles, and acceptance criteria. A specification should therefore name the applicable standard and qualification level rather than simply request a “low-emission valve.”

Selection question Why it matters
Which standard and edition apply? A general claim may not define the test method or leakage limit.
Was the complete valve tested or only the packing? Packing qualification alone does not prove assembled-valve performance.
Do the test conditions resemble service? Qualification is most useful when temperature, pressure, and cycling are relevant.
Is production testing required? Type approval confirms a design, not every supplied unit.
What maintenance instructions apply? Field performance depends on installation, adjustment, and inspection.

Certification should not be read as a guarantee of zero leakage throughout the valve’s service life. It demonstrates performance under defined test conditions. Actual results still depend on the process medium, thermal cycles, installation quality, operating frequency, maintenance, and damage after commissioning.

How Fugitive Emissions Change Valve Selection

The selection process should begin with the fluid. Engineers need to know whether it is volatile, toxic, flammable, odorous, regulated, or commercially valuable. Pressure and temperature alone are insufficient; cycling frequency, thermal variation, vibration, actuator loads, and maintenance access also influence sealing reliability.

Valve architecture matters. Rising stems, rotating shafts, bolted bonnets, welded joints, packing systems, and bellows seals have different performance and maintenance characteristics. Bellows-sealed globe valves, for example, can add a containment barrier for selected hazardous services, but they are not a universal replacement for packed valves. Compatibility, cycle life, pressure-temperature limits, inspection needs, and failure modes still require review.

Material selection should extend beyond the body to the stem finish, packing chemistry, gaskets, fasteners, and sealing interfaces. Procurement documents should define required certificates, traceability, testing records, and approved deviations.

Lifecycle planning is equally important. Facilities should establish baseline inspections, prioritize hazardous services, record recurring leaks, and feed field data into future specifications. Repeated repair of the same valve type under similar conditions may indicate that the original selection basis needs revision.

FAQ

1. Are fugitive emissions the same as visible valve leakage?

No. Fugitive emissions may be invisible and detectable only with portable analyzers, optical gas-imaging equipment, or other instruments.

2. Do all industrial valves need low-emission certification?

Not necessarily. The need depends on the fluid, regulations, facility standards, risk level, and customer specification. Low-volatility water service does not usually require the same controls as methane, VOC, toxic, or hazardous-gas service.

3. Is API 624 equivalent to ISO 15848-1?

No. Both address emissions performance, but their scopes, procedures, classifications, and acceptance criteria differ. The correct standard depends on valve type and project requirements.

4. Does certified packing make a complete valve low-emission?

Not by itself. Stem geometry, stuffing-box dimensions, surface finish, gland loading, body joints, manufacturing quality, and assembly all influence the completed valve.

5. Can maintenance compensate for poor valve selection?

Maintenance can reduce leakage, but it cannot always overcome an unsuitable design, incompatible materials, excessive cycling, or operation outside the intended range.

6. Should the lowest measured leakage determine the purchase?

Not alone. Emissions performance must be balanced with pressure containment, shutoff requirements, corrosion resistance, operability, fire safety where relevant, maintainability, availability, and lifecycle cost.

Conclusion

Fugitive emissions are turning valve selection from a mainly mechanical decision into an environmental, economic, and operational one. The strongest approach is not to add “low emission” as a vague datasheet requirement, but to connect service conditions with a relevant test standard, documented qualification, appropriate materials, production controls, and a practical maintenance plan.
