PET Food-Contact Compliance: A Comprehensive Guide for Importers on FDA And EU Requirements

Feb 25, 2026

In the global food and beverage industry, Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET) is the gold standard for packaging due to its clarity, strength, and recyclability. However, for importers, the regulatory landscape is a shifting mosaic of safety standards and environmental mandates. Navigating the nuances between the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Union (EU) regulations is no longer just a legal hurdle—it is a competitive necessity.

Understanding the Core Differences: FDA vs. EU Regulations

While both the FDA and the EU share the goal of protecting human health, their methodologies for PET compliance differ significantly. For an importer, "compliance in one region" does not guarantee "compliance in the other."

The FDA Framework (United States)

The FDA regulates PET under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. PET is generally considered a "food additive" if its components might migrate into food.

  • FCN (Food Contact Notification): Most new PET additives or polymers enter the market through an FCN. Once an FCN is effective, it is often specific to the manufacturer mentioned in the notification.
  • GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe): Some substances used in PET production have historical data supporting their safety.
  • Threshold of Regulation (TOR): If the migration level is extremely low (below 0.5 ppb), a formal food additive petition may not be required.

The EU Framework (European Union)

The EU approach is more centralized and prescriptive, primarily governed by Regulation (EU) No 10/2011 (the "Plastics Regulation").

  • The Union List: Only substances (monomers, additives) listed in Annex I of 10/2011 can be used.
  • SML (Specific Migration Limits): The EU sets strict limits on how much of a specific chemical (e.g., Antimony) can leach into food.
  • OML (Overall Migration Limit): A general limit of 10 mg/dm² of the food contact surface is applied to all plastic materials.

The Critical Role of Migration Testing for Importers

As an importer, you are legally responsible for the safety of the products you bring into your market. You cannot simply take a supplier’s word; you must understand the Migration Test Report.

Overall Migration (OML) vs. Specific Migration (SML)

  • OML measures the total amount of non-volatile substances that transfer from the PET to the food simulant. It is an indicator of the material's inertness.
  • SML focuses on specific toxicological hazards. For PET, the primary concern is often Antimony trioxide (used as a catalyst) and Acetaldehyde (a byproduct of PET degradation that affects taste).

Simulants and Conditions

Testing must reflect real-world use. If you are importing PET bottles for orange juice (acidic), the testing must use Simulant B (3% Acetic Acid). If the product will be hot-filled, the test temperature must reflect that thermal stress.

The 2024-2026 Hot Topic: PFAS and "Forever Chemicals"

A major shift in both EU and US policy is the aggressive phase-out of PFAS (Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances). While PET itself does not typically contain PFAS, these chemicals are sometimes used as processing aids or in coatings to prevent sticking.

Importers Beware: Several U.S. states (like California and New York) and EU member states are moving toward total bans on PFAS in food packaging. By 2026, many experts predict that "PFAS-Free" certifications will be a mandatory requirement for any PET importer.

Recycled PET (rPET): The Importer’s New Compliance Frontier

With the world moving toward a circular economy, rPET is in high demand. However, it carries higher risks because the "history" of the plastic is unknown.

  • FDA Approach: Focuses on the "Challenge Test." The recycler must prove their process can remove contaminants from a "worst-case" contaminated stream.
  • EU Regulation 2022/1616: This is a game-changer. It requires all rPET used in food contact to come from a process specifically authorized by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA). It also mandates that 100% of the input must be food-grade PET collected in a "closed loop" or a controlled collection system.

Actionable Advice for Importers: If you are importing rPET containers, you must request the "Letter of No Objection" (LNO) for the US market or the "EFSA Register Number" for the EU market.

Comprehensive Importer Checklist: Avoiding Customs Rejections

To ensure smooth entry into the market, follow this procedural roadmap:

  1. Verify the Material: Ensure the PET grade is Virgin or EFSA/FDA-approved rPET.
  2. Request the DoC: The Declaration of Compliance is the most important document. It must list the SMLs and verify that the product meets 10/2011 (EU) or 21 CFR (FDA) standards.
  3. Audit the Lab: Ensure the testing was done by an ISO 17025 accredited laboratory.
  4. Batch Traceability: Ensure each shipment has a batch number that links back to the specific production date and raw material source.
  5. Review the Testing Conditions: Does the test cover "Hot-fill," "Microwave," or "Long-term storage" if your product requires it?
  6. Sensory Testing: For PET, ensure an "Organoleptic Test" has been performed to guarantee the plastic doesn't change the taste or smell of the food.
  7. PFAS-Free Statement: Proactively ask for a statement confirming no intentionally added PFAS.
  8. Heavy Metal Compliance: Check for Lead, Cadmium, Mercury, and Hexavalent Chromium levels (TPCH in the US or 94/62/EC in the EU).
  9. Labeling Requirements: Check for recycling symbols (the PET 1 logo) and "For Food Contact" symbols (the glass/fork icon in the EU).

FAQ: Expert Answers to Common Importer Questions

Q1: Is PET BPA-free?

Yes. Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET) does not use Bisphenol A (BPA) in its production. BPA is primarily found in polycarbonates and epoxy resins. However, importers should still request a "BPA-Free" statement to satisfy consumer demand and avoid confusion in marketing.

Q2: Does FDA approval guarantee EU compliance?

No. While the FDA might allow a specific additive based on an FCN, that same additive might not be on the EU Union List (10/2011) or might have a much lower Specific Migration Limit (SML) in Europe. You must verify compliance for each specific jurisdiction separately.

Q3: What is "Acetaldehyde" and why should importers care?

Acetaldehyde is a naturally occurring substance, but in PET manufacturing, it can form as a thermal degradation product. While not dangerous in small amounts, it can migrate into water or soda and give it a "sweet, fruity" or "plastic" off-flavor. For beverage importers, testing for Acetaldehyde is essential for maintaining brand quality.

Q4: How often should I re-test my PET products?

Regulatory experts suggest that testing should be redone whenever there is a change in the resin supplier, the manufacturing process, or the packaging design. Even without changes, a "periodic validation" every 12 to 24 months is a best practice for high-volume importers.

Q5: Can I import PET made from 100% recycled content?

Yes, provided the recycling process has been validated. For the U.S., look for an FDA LNO (Letter of No Objection). For the EU, ensure the recycler is compliant with Regulation 2022/1616. Be aware that 100% rPET may have a slightly yellow or grey tint compared to virgin PET.

Navigating the 2026 Shift

Ensuring PET materials meet FDA and EU food-contact standards is essential for importers looking to maintain product safety, regulatory compliance, and consumer trust. By understanding migration limits, documentation requirements, and traceability obligations, importers can proactively prevent compliance issues and costly recalls. Ultimately, a thorough approach to PET food-contact compliance not only safeguards end users but also strengthens your supply chain, mitigates legal risk, and positions your brand as a reliable partner in the global food industry.
