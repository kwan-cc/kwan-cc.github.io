4-Axis vs. 5-Axis CNC Rotary Tables: Matching the Right Configuration to Your Machining Requirements

Jun 24, 2026

The decision to add a CNC rotary table to an existing machining center — or to specify one as part of a new machine acquisition — is one of the more consequential investments a precision machining operation can make. Done correctly, it enables multi-sided part completion in a single setup, eliminates fixture changeover time, and opens machining capability to complex geometries that would otherwise require dedicated 5-axis machines or multiple setups.

Done incorrectly, a rotary table becomes an expensive accessory that adds setup complexity without proportional output improvement.

This guide clarifies the key differences between 4th axis rotary tables and 5-axis tilting rotary tables, identifies the application scenarios where each excels, and provides a practical framework for specifying the right configuration.

Defining the Axes: What 4-Axis and 5-Axis Mean for Rotary Tables

Before comparing configurations, it helps to clarify the axis notation in context:

A standard 3-axis vertical machining center controls motion in X (left-right), Y (front-back), and Z (up-down). Adding a rotary table introduces additional controlled axes:

4th axis (A-axis): A rotary table mounted with its rotation axis horizontal (typically aligned with the X-axis). The workpiece rotates around a horizontal axis, enabling machining on multiple faces without manually repositioning the part. The 4th axis provides rotational indexing or continuous interpolation with the existing linear axes.

5th axis (B-axis) with tilting: A tilting rotary table adds a second rotational axis (typically tilting in the B-axis direction) to the 4th axis rotation. This enables the workpiece to be positioned at compound angles — rotated and tilted simultaneously — giving the cutting tool access to complex undercuts, angled features, and compound-curved surfaces without the workpiece leaving the machine.

The distinction matters significantly for application matching.

4th Axis Rotary Table: Applications and Advantages

Primary Application Scenarios

Cylindrical and round parts with features at multiple angular positions: Cam followers, index pins, flanged shafts with axial holes, and coupling components requiring precisely positioned holes or slots at defined angular intervals are natural 4th axis applications. A single setup completes angular features that would otherwise require multiple vice repositions.

Long parts requiring machining along their length: Horizontal 4th axis mounting places long workpieces — crankshafts, splined shafts, impeller blanks — along the X-axis, allowing the spindle to access features along the full part length with a single clamp.

Indexing for multi-face prismatic parts: Cube and rectangular workpieces with machined features on four or more faces can be rotated 90° between cuts, replacing the time and error risk of manually refixturing. This is particularly effective in small-batch job shop environments where setup time represents a significant fraction of total part cost.

Integration with existing VMCs: A 4th axis rotary table can be mounted on the table of most vertical machining centers without significant machine modification, making it an economical capability expansion for shops that cannot justify a full 5-axis machine.

Key Specifications to Evaluate

Table diameter and through-bore diameter: Larger table diameters support heavier, larger parts. Through-bore diameter determines maximum workholding mandrel size for hollow shaft parts.

Maximum table load (kg/lbs): The clamped workpiece plus fixture weight must stay within the rated load. Exceeding table load ratings creates positioning errors and accelerates worm drive wear.

Positioning accuracy and repeatability: For most production indexing applications, positioning accuracy of ±10 arc-seconds and repeatability of ±5 arc-seconds is adequate. High-precision applications in aerospace, medical, and optics may require ±3 arc-seconds or better — specifications that should be verified against the manufacturer's test certificate, not just catalog claims.

Drive mechanism: Worm and wheel drives remain dominant for 4th axis tables due to their high torque multiplication and self-locking characteristics. Dual-lead worm designs enable backlash compensation adjustment through table life. Direct-drive (torque motor) 4th axis tables eliminate gear backlash entirely but carry a significant price premium.

5-Axis Tilting Rotary Table: Applications and Advantages

Primary Application Scenarios

Impellers, bladed disks, and turbine components: These parts feature complex compound-angle blade profiles that cannot be machined effectively with 4+1 (indexed) positioning. True 5-axis simultaneous interpolation allows the cutting tool to maintain optimal orientation relative to a constantly changing surface normal — the fundamental requirement for efficient impeller machining.

Medical implants and orthopedic components: Hip cup acetabular components, femoral stems, and spinal implants involve compound curved geometries in difficult materials (Ti-6Al-4V, cobalt-chrome). 5-axis simultaneous machining maintains consistent chip load on complex contours and enables shorter, more rigid tool engagement for better surface finish in these demanding materials.

Aerospace structural components: Aluminum airframe ribs, fittings, and brackets with multiple machined faces at compound angles are natural candidates for 5-axis tilting tables. The ability to position the workpiece optimally for each feature reduces tool length requirements and improves surface quality.

Mold cavities with deep undercuts: Mold and die makers working on complex injection mold cavities benefit from 5-axis tilting capability when cavity geometry includes drafted walls, undercuts, and complex parting surface profiles.

Configuration Options for 5-Axis Tilting Tables

Trunnion-style (fork-type) tilting rotary tables clamp the rotary table on both sides, providing high rigidity suitable for heavier workpieces and more aggressive cutting. The trunnion configuration limits X-axis travel on the host machine but delivers excellent rigidity for titanium and steel machining.

Swivel head tilting tables are more compact and preserve more of the host machine's travel envelope, but typically carry lower load ratings. They are well-suited for aluminum aerospace machining where part weight is moderate.

CNC manual tilting tables offer an intermediate solution: the tilt axis is manually set and locked (rather than CNC-controlled), while the rotation axis (C-axis) remains CNC-driven. This hybrid approach enables multi-face access at pre-set angles without the full investment of a simultaneous 5-axis tilting system. It is particularly cost-effective for shops that need angular workpiece positioning but not true simultaneous 5-axis contouring.

Decision Framework: Choosing Between 4th Axis and 5-Axis Tilting

Use a 4th axis rotary table when:

  • Your dominant work involves cylindrical parts, round bar stock, or prismatic parts requiring multi-face access at 90° intervals
  • Budget or machine compatibility limits full 5-axis configuration
  • Part geometries do not require compound angle simultaneous contouring
  • You are adding rotary capability to an existing 3-axis VMC

Use a 5-axis tilting rotary table when:

  • Parts include compound-angle surfaces, undercuts, or blade profiles requiring simultaneous 5-axis interpolation
  • You are machining titanium, cobalt-chrome, or other aerospace/medical alloys where tool orientation control is critical for surface quality and tool life
  • Part complexity warrants the investment in a dedicated 5-axis machining cell
  • Your customers include aerospace or medical device OEMs with explicit 5-axis capability requirements

FAQ

Q: Can I use a 4th axis tilting table for 5-axis machining on my existing 3-axis VMC? A: With a CNC tilting rotary table, you can achieve 5-axis positioning (5-axis indexed or "3+2" machining), where the table tilts and rotates to position the workpiece, and then the 3 linear axes perform the cutting while the rotary axes remain stationary. This is highly effective for parts that need multi-face access without true simultaneous 5-axis contouring. True simultaneous 5-axis interpolation requires a machine controller capable of coordinating all 5 axes simultaneously — typically a full 5-axis CNC controller, which some VMCs can be retrofitted to support.

Q: How does backlash in the rotary table affect part accuracy? A: Backlash — the clearance between mating gear teeth — creates positioning error when the table reverses direction. In indexed applications where positioning occurs in one direction only, backlash has minimal practical effect. In continuous contouring applications where the table reverses direction during a cut, uncompensated backlash creates dimensional and form errors. High-quality tables address this through dual-lead worm geometry (adjustable backlash) or direct-drive torque motors (zero backlash). When evaluating accuracy specifications, confirm whether the stated accuracy represents one-directional or bi-directional performance.

Q: What is the difference between a CNC rotary table and a CNC index table? A: A CNC rotary table provides continuous (infinitely variable) angular positioning with full servo control — the A or C axis functions as a true programmable axis. A CNC index table positions at a fixed set of discrete angles (e.g., every 5°, 2.5°, or 1°) using a curvic coupling or Hirth coupling for locking. Index tables provide higher clamping rigidity at their indexed positions but cannot position at arbitrary angles. For applications requiring specific fixed-angle indexing at maximum rigidity — gear machining, spline cutting — index tables are preferred. For general-purpose rotary positioning and contouring, full CNC rotary tables are more flexible.

Conclusion

The choice between a 4th axis rotary table and a 5-axis tilting rotary table comes down to the complexity of your dominant workpiece geometry, your host machine's controller capability, and the investment level that production volume can justify. For most general-purpose job shops and CNC machining centers in the light-to-medium machining segment, a quality 4th axis rotary table delivers significant productivity gains at moderate cost. Operations targeting aerospace, medical, or complex mold and die work should evaluate 5-axis tilting configurations that enable full geometric access without workpiece repositioning.
