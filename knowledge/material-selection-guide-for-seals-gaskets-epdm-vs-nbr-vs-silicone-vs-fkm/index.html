Material Selection Guide for Seals & Gaskets: EPDM vs NBR vs Silicone vs FKM

Aug 05, 2026

Elastomer selection is often treated as a “materials choice,” but in sealing engineering it functions as a compatibility and aging-engineering problem. EPDM, NBR, silicone, and FKM are four common elastomer families with different polymer chemistries, which strongly influence swelling, hardness/elasticity change, and long-term degradation under real service conditions.

This guide provides an engineer-oriented selection workflow you can apply to rubber gaskets and seals by matching media, temperature, and exposure environment, then validating the expected aging behavior using practical confirmation steps.

1) Build a compatibility map from your application requirements

Start by capturing the variables that drive elastomer performance:

  • Media: oils/fuels/solvents vs. water/steam vs. mixed or contaminated fluids
  • Temperature profile: minimum/maximum, continuous vs. cycling, dwell time near peaks
  • Exposure environment: indoor vs. outdoor; ozone/UV; heat aging; humidity
  • Seal type & mechanics: static gasket compression vs. dynamic motion; surface finish and contact pressure
  • Acceptance targets: allowable leakage rate, max set, allowable hardness change, target life

Most failures that appear “mysterious” in the field are traceable to one missing variable in this list—especially concentration and temperature-time history. Multiple industry material-selection references emphasize that elastomer compatibility must be checked against the specific media and service conditions rather than relying only on general polymer labels.

2) Elastomer families: expected strengths and typical engineering caveats

EPDM (ethylene propylene diene monomer)

Engineering positioning

  • Frequently selected for weathering/ozone/UV exposure and for water/steam-oriented sealing environments.
  • When the dominant aging drivers are outdoor exposure and oxidative degradation, EPDM compounds are commonly chosen as a practical first line.

Caveat

  • For hydrocarbon fuels and many petroleum-based oils, EPDM is often not the first choice because swelling and property changes can dominate unless the compound and application are validated.

NBR (nitrile/buna-N)

Engineering positioning

  • Commonly used for sealing against many oil and petroleum fluid exposures in practical industrial applications.
  • In many designs, NBR becomes the baseline elastomer for cost-effective oil/fuel sealing—again, compound-dependent.

Caveat

  • If outdoor ozone/UV exposure is part of the requirement, NBR selection may require additional compound/cover strategy or reconsideration depending on the failure mode observed.

Silicone (VMQ)

Engineering positioning

  • Often chosen where temperature cycling and elasticity retention across a wide temperature window matter.
  • Can be beneficial when design margins depend on maintaining flexibility and sealing contact across temperature excursions.

Caveat

  • Silicone is not universally ideal for fuel/oil environments; swelling and softening behavior depend strongly on the specific formulation and media. Validation is required when fuels or solvents are involved.

FKM (fluorocarbon; often referenced with “Viton” in industry)

Engineering positioning

  • Selected for chemical resistance and higher-temperature performance in aggressive environments where other elastomers show unacceptable swelling or degradation.
  • Frequently used when the failure driver is chemical attack plus thermal aging.

Caveat

  • Media compatibility depends on FKM grade/compound and the exact chemicals present; treat FKM as “premium but not universal” and confirm with test evidence for your specific fluid.

A concise engineering takeaway

A material pairwise “swap” that frequently works in practice is:

  • Fuel/oil dominated → NBR or FKM
  • Water/steam + outdoor weathering dominated → EPDM
  • Temperature cycling/flexibility dominated → silicone
  • Harsh chemical + higher temperature dominated → FKM

This kind of mapping is consistent with how elastomer families are commonly organized in industry references, and it helps teams form a shortlist before testing.

3) Engineering validation: confirm compatibility with measurable criteria

Instead of relying on polymer-family reputation, confirm using testable checkpoints that correlate with sealing performance:

A) Swell and hardness change (direct indicator for leakage risk)

For the relevant elastomer compound:

  • Soak specimens in the same media (or representative mixture) at the expected temperature(s)
  • Measure mass/volume change (swelling) and hardness change
  • Track whether the compound shifts toward conditions that reduce sealing force (compression set, softening) or increases leakage risk (loss of resilience)

This step addresses the core mechanism of most rubber sealing failures: property drift under chemical/thermal exposure.

B) Compression set / relaxation behavior (static gasket reliability)

For gasket designs:

  • Evaluate compression set under representative temperature and time
  • Correlate compression set to your stack-up assumptions (bolt preload, flange finish, and gasket thickness)

Compression-set-driven leakage is common in static sealing when the compound softens or loses elasticity due to exposure.

C) Ozone/UV aging tests (for outdoor seals)

If ozone/UV is a driver:

  • run accelerated ozone exposure on representative compound
  • evaluate cracking and mechanical property retention

This is especially relevant for elastomers that are chosen primarily for outdoor durability.

D) Seal-function validation (the final acceptance gate)

Even with material tests, sealing behavior also depends on geometry and mechanics:

  • prototype the seal/gasket geometry
  • validate leakage under pressure/temperature cycling and real contact pressure conditions

For production decisions, functional tests are the strongest evidence.

4) How to document the decision (so it survives design reviews)

For an engineering team, the output should be traceable. A good engineering “material selection record” includes:

  • Service conditions: media, temp profile, exposure environment, contamination assumptions
  • Candidate elastomers: EPDM/NBR/silicone/FKM (and compound notes)
  • Failure-mode hypothesis: swelling, softening/compression set, oxidative cracking, chemical attack
  • Test plan and acceptance criteria: swell %, hardness change limits, compression set targets, ozone/UV cracking thresholds
  • Results and selection: measured outcomes and rationale

5) Bottom line

A robust seal/gasket material selection process is:

  1. classify the dominant exposure driver (media chemistry, temperature-time history, ozone/UV exposure, and mechanics)
  2. shortlist EPDM/NBR/silicone/FKM based on that classification
  3. validate compatibility via swell/hardness, compression set (for gaskets), and ozone/UV (when relevant)
  4. confirm with functional leakage testing using your actual geometry and contact pressure

That workflow produces decisions that engineering teams can defend in design reviews and that correlate with field performance.
